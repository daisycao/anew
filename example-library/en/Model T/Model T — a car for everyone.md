# Model T — a car for everyone

Research question: how did Ford make a car everyone could buy? In 1909 a car was a rich man's toy. By 1917 Ford sold 785,432 a year at $360. What did it: the product, the price, the factory or the people?

**Short answer, as of version 4:** the price came first. Ford named a price most families could pay, then the single model, the moving line and the $5 day were all ways of making that price true. (Version 1 said "the assembly line". Reading Ford's own account changed that.)

## 1. One model, no options #product

> "Any customer can have a car painted any colour that he wants so long as it is black." —— Ford, 1922, about 1909

👩 He cut the menu to one dish, and sales hated it. You can't build one line for eight models, so this decision comes first.

> [!claude]
> Agreed, and the dates back you up: one model in 1909, the first moving line in 1913.
> One check on the famous line: Ford dates it to 1909, but black-only is usually dated to about 1914–1925, and early Model Ts came in other colours. Black arrived with the line; the usual explanation is that it dried fastest. Read the quote as Ford looking back in 1922, not a record of 1909.

## 2. Price first, cost follows #business

> "Therefore we first reduce the price to a point where we believe more sales will result. Then we go ahead and try to make the price." —— Ford, 1922

👩 Most companies do cost + margin = price. He did it backwards: name the price, then force the cost down to meet it. "No one knows what a cost ought to be." That's the line I keep thinking about.

| Year | Touring car | Cars made |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1909–10 | $950 | 18,664 |
| 1911–12 | $690 | 78,440 |
| 1913–14 | $550 | 248,307 |
| 1916–17 | $360 | 785,432 |

Price −62%, output ×42 in seven years (Ford's own table).

> [!claude]
> That is his stated policy, and his table fits it. What the table can't show is which came first: price and volume moved together, and "price led" is Ford's account. To call it proven you'd want an outside source, such as trade-press prices by year. Logged under *Still open*.

## 3. Bring the work to the men: the moving line #tech

> "The first step forward in assembly came when we began taking the work to the men instead of the men to the work." —— Ford, 1922

👩 Magneto: about 20 minutes → 5. Chassis: 12h28m → 1h33m. Same parts, same men. Only the movement changed.

> [!claude]
> Both numbers are in the book. Worth adding how carefully it was tuned: 60 inches a minute was too fast, 18 too slow, they settled on 44. "Every second necessary but not a single unnecessary second." The line wasn't installed, it was tested.

## 4. The $5 day was buying stability, not PR #team

> "it had been necessary to hire at the rate of about 53,000 a year in order to keep a constant force of 14,000" —— Ford, 1922

👩 Version 2 said PR stunt. I changed my mind: hiring 53,000 people a year to keep 14,000 is chaos. The line made the work dull, so people left; the $5 was the price of keeping them. (In 1915 they hired 6,508.)

> [!claude]
> That reading fits the numbers he gives. Two things to keep next to it:
> - It wasn't $5 for everyone. It was wage plus a profit share, after six months, for workers who met the company's conditions on family and habits; 60% qualified at first, 87% after a year.
> - The turnover figures are Ford's own. Historians broadly agree turnover was very high in 1913, but the exact 53,000 comes only from him.

## 5. Taylor timed the man; Ford moved the work #tech

> "In the past the man has been first; in the future the system must be first." —— Taylor, 1911

👩 Taylor stands next to Schmidt with a watch: "Now pick up a pig and walk. Now sit down and rest." Ford doesn't need the watch: the line sets the pace. Both take the thinking out of the job.

> [!claude]
> Your contrast holds, and Ford says the quiet part himself: the aim is "the reduction of the necessity for thought on the part of the worker".
> One caution on Taylor: the pig-iron story is his own retelling, and later historians (Wrege and Perroni, 1974) argued he tidied it up. Use it as Taylor's claim, not as a measured result.

## 6. Our pricing: name the price first #for-me

👩 (from my retell) We priced our app as cost + margin and landed at $24 a month. Nobody we asked would pay more than $10. Try Ford's order: start from $10 and ask what would have to change to make it work.

> [!claude]
> Worth trying, with Ford's own condition attached: it worked because volume could grow 42×. If our market can't grow like that, a lower price only means lower revenue. See [[Our pricing]].

## Ford vs Taylor, side by side

| | Taylor, 1911 | Ford, 1922 | What changed | What didn't |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unit of work | One man, one task | One station on a moving line | The work moves, not the man | Work is split into small, timed motions |
| Who sets the pace | A man with a stopwatch | The speed of the line | Control moves from a person to the machine | The worker doesn't choose |
| Pay | +60% for hitting the task | $5 day after six months | From a reward per task to a reason to stay | High pay buys compliance |
| Proof offered | One story (Schmidt) | A table of prices and output | Ford gives more numbers | Both are the author's own account |

## In their shoes: you run sales at Ford, 1909

> [!claude] In their shoes
> It's 1909. Cars are sold as luxury "pleasure cars". Ford has already built eight different models. Your dealers want more choice, not less. Ford announces: one model, one chassis, and the price will go down every year. The general comment: "If Ford does that he will be out of business in six months."
> Do you back him, or argue for a range?

👩 (my answer, Oct 7) I'd argue for two models: a cheap one for volume, a nicer one for margin. One car for everyone sounds like ignoring the customer.

> [!claude] What happened
> Ford kept one model for nearly two decades, and output went from 18,664 (1909–10) to 1.25 million (1920–21). Then your argument won: by the mid-1920s General Motors was selling "a car for every purse and purpose", Chevrolet outsold Ford in 1927, and the Model T was dropped that year.
> So you weren't wrong, just about fifteen years early. One model wins while most people don't have a car yet; choice wins once they do.
